**96 concepts**, **687 independently tracked subsections**, across **19 learning objectives**. Includes the uploaded Digestive System, Urinary System, and Musculoskeletal & Immobility slide decks.

Every subsection has its own multiple-choice or select-all question, rationale, exposure history and independent 10/20/50 review. A concept is mastered only when every subsection is mastered.

## Coverage and subsection outlines

### GI-1: GI symptoms, stool, and acid-base balance (4 concepts)

**Vomiting and metabolic alkalosis** — Digestive slides 3, 6

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Loss of gastric hydrochloric acid predisposes to metabolic alkalosis.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Pregnancy, GI infection, medications, motion sickness, and gastric distension can trigger emesis.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Vomiting removes gastric hydrochloric acid, increasing the relative bicarbonate concentration and producing metabolic alkalosis.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Nausea, retching, emesis, dehydration, and possible hypochloremia accompany repeated vomiting.
- **Complications and consequences:** Severe fluid and chloride loss can cause hypovolemia and electrolyte abnormalities.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** An alkalemic pH with elevated bicarbonate supports metabolic alkalosis; assess electrolytes and hydration.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Replace fluids and electrolytes and treat the reason for vomiting.

**Diarrhea and metabolic acidosis** — Digestive slides 4-5

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Diarrheal loss of bicarbonate can cause a normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Infection, medications, malabsorption, and intestinal inflammation may cause diarrhea.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Loss of bicarbonate-rich intestinal fluid can produce normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Frequent loose watery stools, cramping, and dehydration are common.
- **Complications and consequences:** Hypovolemia, electrolyte depletion, and acid-base imbalance can develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Assess stool pattern, hydration, electrolytes, and acid-base status; test stool when indicated.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Rehydration and treatment of the cause take priority; infection-specific precautions may be needed.

**Steatorrhea (fatty stools)** — Digestive slide 5

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Steatorrhea is excessive fat in stool and suggests fat malabsorption.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Pancreatic enzyme deficiency, impaired bile availability, celiac disease, or other malabsorption can cause fatty stools.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Impaired digestion or absorption of lipids leaves excess fat within the stool.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Bulky, greasy, foul-smelling stools that may float are characteristic.
- **Complications and consequences:** Fat-soluble vitamin deficiency and weight loss may follow chronic fat malabsorption.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Stool fat assessment and evaluation of pancreatic or small-bowel disease help identify the cause.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Treat the malabsorptive disorder and replace missing nutrients or enzymes when indicated.

**Constipation and fecal impaction** — Digestive slide 4

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Opioids, dehydration, immobility, low fiber intake, and neurologic disease predispose to constipation.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Slow colonic transit or impaired evacuation increases stool water absorption and hardness.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Hard or infrequent stools, straining, abdominal fullness, and difficult defecation occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Hemorrhoids, fecal impaction, or vasovagal syncope from straining can occur.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Compare with a person's normal bowel pattern and review medications and hydration.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Promote fluids when appropriate, fiber, mobility, and individualized bowel regimen.

### GI-2: Esophageal, reflux, and peptic conditions (8 concepts)

**Achalasia and dysphagia** — Digestive slides 7-8

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Achalasia causes impaired esophageal peristalsis and insufficient LES relaxation.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Degeneration of esophageal inhibitory neurons causes primary achalasia; mechanical obstruction can cause dysphagia for other reasons.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Failure of lower esophageal sphincter relaxation plus impaired peristalsis causes esophageal retention.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Dysphagia to both solids and liquids, regurgitation, and esophageal distension are typical.
- **Complications and consequences:** Aspiration, weight loss, and esophageal dilation may occur.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Manometry confirms impaired LES relaxation; a contrast swallow may show a narrowed distal esophagus.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Endoscopic dilation, myotomy, or botulinum toxin may reduce LES resistance.

**Hiatal hernia** — Digestive slides 9-10

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** A hiatal hernia is displacement of part of the stomach through the diaphragm.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Age-related tissue laxity and increased intra-abdominal pressure predispose to hiatal hernia.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** A portion of stomach moves through the diaphragm into the thoracic cavity.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Heartburn, belching, retrosternal discomfort, and worsening after lying down may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** A paraesophageal hernia can incarcerate or strangulate, unlike an uncomplicated sliding hernia.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** A contrast swallow or endoscopy can demonstrate herniation and associated reflux injury.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Manage reflux symptoms; large or complicated paraesophageal hernias may need repair.

**Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)** — Digestive slides 11-12

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Head-of-bed elevation reduces nighttime reflux exposure; routine stacking of pillows may not elevate the torso appropriately.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Obesity, hiatal hernia, large late meals, and reduced LES competence increase reflux risk.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Transient LES relaxation or weak sphincter tone permits gastric contents to contact distal esophageal mucosa.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Pyrosis, sour regurgitation, nocturnal symptoms, and worsening while supine are typical.
- **Complications and consequences:** Erosive esophagitis, stricture, and Barrett esophagus can develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Often diagnosed clinically; endoscopy is used for alarm symptoms or complications.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Elevate the head of bed, avoid late meals, and use acid suppression when indicated.

**Duodenal peptic ulcer** — Digestive slides 13, 15, 18

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Duodenal ulcer pain classically occurs when the stomach is empty and may improve with food or antacids.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Helicobacter pylori infection and NSAID use are common causes of peptic ulcer disease.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Acid-peptic damage breaches the protective duodenal mucosal lining.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Epigastric pain classically occurs 2 to 3 hours after meals and may improve with food or antacids.
- **Complications and consequences:** Bleeding, penetration, perforation, or outlet obstruction can occur.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Upper endoscopy and testing for H. pylori can establish the diagnosis and cause.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Eradicate H. pylori when present, avoid NSAIDs if possible, and use acid suppression.

**Dysphagia and aspiration risk** — Digestive slides 7-8

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Stroke, neuromuscular disorders, narrowing, and esophageal tumors may impair swallowing.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Impaired oral/pharyngeal coordination or esophageal transport prevents safe bolus transit.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Cough with swallowing, food sticking, regurgitation, or wet voice may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Aspiration pneumonia, dehydration, and malnutrition can follow.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Swallow evaluation and tailored imaging/diagnostic tests identify dysfunction.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Protect airway and use appropriate texture or swallowing strategies based on evaluation.

**Peptic ulcer disease (PUD)** — Digestive slide 13

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Helicobacter pylori and NSAIDs undermine gastroduodenal mucosal defenses.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Acid-pepsin injury erodes protective mucosa, producing an ulcer crater.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Epigastric burning discomfort, dyspepsia, and sometimes occult bleeding occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Hemorrhage, perforation, and gastric outlet obstruction are serious complications.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Upper endoscopy and H. pylori testing identify ulcers and common causes.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Acid suppression and H. pylori eradication when present promote healing.

**Gastric ulcer** — Digestive slide 14

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** NSAID exposure and H. pylori are major causes of gastric ulcers.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Breakdown of stomach mucosal protection allows acidic injury of the gastric wall.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Pain commonly occurs soon after meals; nausea, reduced appetite, and weight loss may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Bleeding, perforation, and possible malignancy requiring evaluation are concerns.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Endoscopy with biopsy as appropriate evaluates a suspicious gastric ulcer.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Treat H. pylori, stop ulcerogenic drugs if possible, and suppress acid.

**Gastritis** — Digestive slide 16

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** NSAIDs, H. pylori, alcohol exposure, and severe physiologic stress may inflame gastric mucosa.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Inflammation damages the protective gastric surface and may impair mucosal function.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Epigastric discomfort, nausea, vomiting, or asymptomatic inflammation can occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Erosion, bleeding, and chronic atrophic changes are possible depending on cause.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** History, H. pylori testing, and endoscopy for concerning symptoms may be used.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Remove the offending trigger and use acid suppression or organism-directed therapy as indicated.

### GI-3: Inflammatory and acute intestinal disorders (7 concepts)

**Crohn disease and transmural inflammation** — Digestive slides 20-24

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Crohn disease may involve the full thickness of the bowel wall and produce skip lesions.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Immune dysregulation, genetic susceptibility, and smoking are associated with Crohn disease.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Patchy skip lesions may involve any GI segment and extend through the entire intestinal wall.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Abdominal pain, chronic diarrhea, weight loss, and malabsorption; terminal ileum is often involved.
- **Complications and consequences:** Transmural inflammation can cause fistulas, strictures, abscesses, and obstruction.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Endoscopy with biopsy and cross-sectional imaging identify distribution and complications.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Control inflammation with appropriate immunomodulating treatment and address nutritional deficits.

**Ulcerative colitis and mucosal inflammation** — Digestive slides 20-24

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Ulcerative colitis typically has continuous inflammation of the colonic mucosa.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Immune-mediated colonic inflammation occurs in genetically susceptible patients.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Continuous mucosal inflammation begins at the rectum and extends proximally through the colon.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Bloody diarrhea, urgency, cramping, and mucus in stool are characteristic.
- **Complications and consequences:** Toxic megacolon, major bleeding, anemia, and increased colorectal cancer risk may develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Colonoscopy with biopsy shows continuous colonic mucosal disease.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Anti-inflammatory or immune-targeted therapy is used; colectomy can be curative for colonic disease.

**Diverticulitis** — Digestive slides 25-26

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Diverticulitis refers to inflamed diverticula; uncomplicated diverticulosis may be asymptomatic.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Diverticulosis, age, and other factors associated with colonic diverticula predispose to diverticulitis.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Inflammation of diverticular outpouchings may produce localized infection.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Left lower quadrant pain, fever, nausea, and leukocytosis are common.
- **Complications and consequences:** Perforation, abscess, fistula, or peritonitis can occur.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** CT abdomen/pelvis helps confirm inflammation and assess for complications.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Supportive care and selective antibiotics; complicated disease may need drainage or surgery.

**Acute appendicitis** — Digestive slides 27-28

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Appendicitis often begins with visceral periumbilical pain before localizing to the right lower quadrant.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Luminal obstruction by a fecalith or lymphoid tissue can trigger appendicitis.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Obstructed outflow increases pressure, reduces perfusion, and can cause ischemia, bacterial invasion, and rupture.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Pain often begins periumbilically then localizes to the right lower quadrant with guarding or rebound.
- **Complications and consequences:** Rupture can cause diffuse peritonitis, sepsis, and hypotension.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** History, exam, leukocytosis, and imaging support diagnosis.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Urgent surgical assessment, IV fluids, and antibiotics as clinically indicated.

**Intestinal obstruction** — Digestive slide 19

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Adhesions, hernias, tumors, and strictures can mechanically obstruct intestinal flow.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Blockage accumulates gas and fluid proximally and can compromise bowel perfusion.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Distention, vomiting, colicky pain, and reduced passage of flatus or stool occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Strangulation, ischemia, perforation, and electrolyte loss are emergencies.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** CT and abdominal assessment help determine site and seriousness.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Bowel rest, IV fluids, decompression when indicated, and urgent surgery for compromised bowel.

**Diverticulosis** — Digestive slide 25

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Age and colonic structural factors predispose to diverticular outpouchings.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Mucosa herniates through weak points in the colonic muscular layer without active inflammation.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Many people are asymptomatic until inflammation or bleeding develops.
- **Complications and consequences:** Diverticulitis or diverticular hemorrhage may occur.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Often identified incidentally on colon imaging or colonoscopy.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Manage bowel health and monitor for new bleeding or inflammation.

**Secondary peritonitis** — Digestive slides 27-28

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Ruptured appendix, perforated ulcer, or other bowel perforation can contaminate the peritoneal cavity.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Bacterial contamination produces widespread peritoneal inflammation and systemic inflammatory response.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Rigid guarding, diffuse severe abdominal pain, fever, tachycardia, and hypotension are concerning.
- **Complications and consequences:** Sepsis, shock, and multi-organ dysfunction can develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Physical examination, labs, and abdominal imaging help locate perforation.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Rapid resuscitation, antibiotics, and urgent source control are necessary.

### GI-4: Hepatic failure and portal complications (3 concepts)

**Portal hypertension and esophageal varices** — Digestive slides 31, 34

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Cirrhotic resistance to portal flow can cause portal hypertension and collateral varices.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Cirrhosis and other causes of increased portal venous resistance lead to portal hypertension.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Fibrotic resistance diverts portal blood into collateral veins, producing enlarged gastroesophageal varices.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Splenomegaly, collateral veins, and signs of chronic liver disease may accompany varices.
- **Complications and consequences:** Variceal rupture can cause life-threatening upper GI bleeding.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Upper endoscopy identifies esophageal varices; imaging and laboratory results assess liver disease.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Portal-pressure reduction and endoscopic variceal therapy may prevent or treat bleeding.

**Hepatic encephalopathy and ammonia** — Digestive slide 33

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Impaired hepatic detoxification and portosystemic shunting can allow ammonia and other neurotoxins to accumulate.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Advanced liver dysfunction, shunting, GI bleeding, constipation, and infection can precipitate encephalopathy.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Portosystemic shunting and impaired hepatic clearance allow neurotoxins such as ammonia to affect brain function.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Confusion, altered sleep-wake cycle, asterixis, and reduced consciousness may develop.
- **Complications and consequences:** Severe encephalopathy can progress to coma and impaired airway protection.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** It is primarily a clinical diagnosis; evaluate triggers and exclude other causes of altered mentation.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Treat precipitating factors; lactulose and sometimes rifaximin reduce gut-derived ammonia burden.

**Ascites in liver disease** — Digestive slides 31, 35

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Ascites is fluid collecting inside the peritoneal cavity.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Cirrhosis with portal hypertension is a common cause of hepatic ascites.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Elevated portal hydrostatic pressure, splanchnic vasodilation, renal sodium retention, and low albumin contribute to peritoneal fluid buildup.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Progressive abdominal distention, shifting dullness, weight gain, and early satiety are common.
- **Complications and consequences:** Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, respiratory restriction, and kidney dysfunction may occur.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Ultrasound confirms fluid; diagnostic paracentesis assesses new or worsening ascites.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Sodium restriction, diuretics, and paracentesis are used when appropriate.

### REN-1: Nephron function, RAAS, and renal testing (5 concepts)

**Glomerular filtration** — Urinary slides 2, 5

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** The glomerular capillary tuft is the filtration structure within the renal corpuscle.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Adequate renal blood flow and intact glomerular capillaries support filtration.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Hydrostatic pressure filters plasma water and small solutes into Bowman space while normally retaining cells and large proteins.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Reduced filtration often produces rising serum creatinine and possible oliguria.
- **Complications and consequences:** Persistent glomerular damage can lead to fluid overload and accumulation of nitrogenous waste.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Estimated GFR and serum creatinine help evaluate overall filtration function.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Preserve perfusion, avoid nephrotoxins, and manage the underlying renal disorder.

**Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) and water reabsorption** — Urinary slide 7

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** ADH increases collecting-duct water permeability through aquaporin insertion.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Increased plasma osmolality or reduced effective circulating volume stimulate ADH secretion.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** ADH acts on collecting ducts to increase water permeability and reabsorption.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Higher ADH activity tends to concentrate urine and reduce its volume.
- **Complications and consequences:** Inappropriate excess ADH can promote dilutional hyponatremia.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Urine osmolality and serum sodium help assess water balance.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Correct the underlying water-balance problem, with fluids tailored to the clinical context.

**Renin and the RAAS response** — Urinary slide 6

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Juxtaglomerular cells release renin, which initiates the RAAS cascade.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Reduced renal perfusion, sympathetic stimulation, or low distal tubular sodium delivery stimulates renin.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Renin initiates angiotensin II production, increasing vascular tone and aldosterone-driven sodium retention.
- **Clinical manifestations:** RAAS activation can raise blood pressure and increase fluid retention.
- **Complications and consequences:** Sustained excessive RAAS activation contributes to hypertension and organ stress.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Assess blood pressure, renal perfusion, electrolytes, and relevant laboratory markers.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Treat the cause; selected kidney and cardiac conditions benefit from RAAS-blocking drugs.

**Proteinuria and the glomerular filtration barrier** — Urinary slides 4, 23-24

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Proteinuria suggests abnormal filtration barrier permeability, though the full differential depends on context.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Diabetes, inflammatory glomerular disease, or other causes of barrier injury produce albumin leakage.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Loss of glomerular size/charge selectivity permits plasma protein to enter urine.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Persistent proteinuria can present as foamy urine and may accompany edema.
- **Complications and consequences:** Severe urinary protein loss contributes to hypoalbuminemia and edema.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Urine albumin-to-creatinine or protein-to-creatinine ratio quantifies protein leakage.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Treat the underlying cause and reduce additional kidney damage.

**Renal diagnostic tests and urinalysis** — Urinary slides 3-4

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Diabetes, hypertension, infections, and obstructive disorders are common reasons for renal testing.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Serum creatinine and eGFR reflect filtration; urinalysis assesses blood, protein, glucose, cells, and sediment.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Elevated creatinine or protein, blood, or WBCs in urine suggests different forms of renal pathology.
- **Complications and consequences:** Missed renal dysfunction can allow worsening electrolyte and fluid complications.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Use creatinine/eGFR, BUN, urinalysis, and urine culture depending on the clinical question.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Interpret serial trends and the clinical condition rather than one isolated result.

### REN-2: Obstruction, stones, and bladder function (10 concepts)

**Urolithiasis (urinary stones)** — Urinary slides 11-12

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** A urinary calculus may produce severe renal colic and blood in the urine.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Dehydration, concentrated urine, metabolic factors, and certain infections predispose to stones.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Supersaturated solutes such as calcium salts or uric acid crystallize in the urinary tract.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Colicky flank pain radiating toward groin, hematuria, nausea, and restlessness are common.
- **Complications and consequences:** Obstruction, hydronephrosis, infection, and kidney injury may occur.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Urinalysis and noncontrast CT often evaluate suspected stones.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Provide analgesia, fluids as appropriate, and relieve infected obstruction urgently.

**Hydronephrosis from urinary obstruction** — Urinary slides 9-10

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Hydronephrosis is distention of the renal collecting system from impaired urinary drainage.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Ureteral stones, strictures, tumors, or outlet obstruction can block urine drainage.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Back pressure dilates the renal pelvis and calyces; persistent pressure can damage kidney parenchyma.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Flank discomfort, urinary changes, or reduced output may occur; it can also be silent.
- **Complications and consequences:** Prolonged bilateral obstruction can cause postrenal acute kidney injury.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Ultrasound can show dilation and help identify obstructive patterns.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Relieve the blockage promptly and treat the underlying cause.

**Stress urinary incontinence** — Urinary slide 13

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Stress incontinence causes leakage when increased abdominal pressure exceeds urethral closure pressure.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Pelvic floor weakness, urethral support loss, and childbirth are risk factors.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Increased abdominal pressure overcomes inadequate urethral closure.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Small urine leaks with coughing, sneezing, jumping, or lifting occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Skin irritation and psychosocial distress may result from persistent leakage.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Symptom history and targeted examination distinguish stress from urge and overflow leakage.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Pelvic-floor muscle training and selected other therapies improve urethral support.

**Urinary tract obstruction** — Urinary slides 9-10

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Stones, strictures, tumors, prostate enlargement, or neurogenic dysfunction can impede urine flow.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Urine accumulates proximal to obstruction, raising pressure and lowering effective renal filtration.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Hesitancy, retention, colicky pain, oliguria, or hydronephrosis may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Persistent obstruction can cause infection and postrenal AKI.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Bladder scan and renal ultrasound help locate urinary retention or hydronephrosis.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Restore drainage and manage the specific obstructive cause.

**Overflow urinary incontinence** — Urinary slide 13

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Prostate enlargement, urethral obstruction, or underactive bladder can cause overflow leakage.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** A chronically overfilled bladder leaks small amounts because emptying is incomplete.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Constant dribbling, weak stream, hesitancy, and high postvoid residual may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Retention predisposes to infection and hydronephrosis.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Postvoid residual measurement identifies incomplete emptying.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Relieve obstruction or improve bladder emptying, often including catheterization when indicated.

**Urge urinary incontinence** — Urinary slide 13

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Detrusor overactivity may occur idiopathically or with neurologic disorders.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Involuntary bladder contractions precede intended voiding.
- **Clinical manifestations:** A sudden compelling urge followed by urine loss and increased frequency is characteristic.
- **Complications and consequences:** Sleep disruption, falls rushing to toilet, and quality-of-life effects can occur.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** History and voiding diary help distinguish urge from stress and overflow leakage.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Bladder training and selected medications can reduce urgency.

**Functional urinary incontinence** — Urinary slide 13

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Impaired mobility, dementia, or environmental barriers interfere with toileting.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** The person may have normal bladder control but cannot access or use a toilet in time.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Urine loss occurs because of difficulty reaching or using toileting facilities.
- **Complications and consequences:** Skin irritation and fall risk may increase.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Assess cognition, mobility, toileting access, and bladder symptoms.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Scheduled toileting and environmental or mobility assistance address the barrier.

**Urinary retention** — Urinary slides 13-14

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Outlet obstruction, neurologic disease, or medications can impair voiding.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Inadequate detrusor emptying leaves an elevated postvoid residual.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Suprapubic fullness, weak stream, hesitancy, or complete inability to void may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** UTI, bladder overdistension, and hydronephrosis can follow.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Bladder scan or catheterized postvoid residual documents retained urine.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Prompt decompression in acute retention and treatment of the cause.

**Urethral stricture** — Urinary slide 14

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Prior urethral injury, instrumentation, surgery, or inflammatory scarring can cause narrowing.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Fibrotic narrowing raises resistance to urinary outflow.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Weak or spraying stream, straining, incomplete emptying, and recurrent infection may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Urinary retention and upstream renal damage are possible.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Uroflowmetry, cystoscopy, or urethral imaging evaluates suspected narrowing.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Urologic dilation or reconstructive intervention depending on severity.

**Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)** — Urinary slides 13-14, 16

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Age-associated benign prostate enlargement can obstruct bladder outlet flow.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Increased outlet resistance impairs bladder emptying and increases residual urine.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Hesitancy, weak stream, nocturia, incomplete emptying, or retention may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Retention, infection, and upper-tract dilation are possible.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Symptom assessment, postvoid residual, and indicated prostate evaluation guide diagnosis.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Behavioral measures, alpha blockers, and selected other therapies help; surgery is used for significant obstruction.

### REN-3: Urinary tract and kidney infections (6 concepts)

**Cystitis** — Urinary slides 15-17

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Cystitis involves lower urinary tract/bladder inflammation.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Ascending bacterial infection, often E. coli, with catheterization or urinary stasis as risks.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Bacteria inflame the bladder mucosa after ascending through the urethra.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic discomfort, and sometimes cloudy urine occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Ascending spread may cause pyelonephritis.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Urinalysis and urine culture when indicated evaluate infection.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Appropriate antimicrobial therapy and addressing predisposing factors treat bacterial cystitis.

**Acute pyelonephritis** — Urinary slides 19, 21

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Pyelonephritis is upper urinary tract infection and more often causes fever and flank/CVA tenderness.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Ascending bacteria, vesicoureteral reflux, obstruction, stones, and catheters predispose to kidney infection.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Ascending infection inflames the renal pelvis and parenchyma.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Fever, chills, flank pain, costovertebral angle tenderness, and sometimes WBC casts occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Bacteremia, sepsis, and renal injury can develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Urinalysis and urine culture help confirm; imaging is considered for complicated cases.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Prompt antibiotics and relief of obstruction when present are key.

**Interstitial cystitis / bladder pain syndrome** — Urinary slide 18

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Interstitial cystitis is chronic bladder pain with urinary symptoms without a routine bacterial UTI.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Its precise cause is uncertain and it is not ordinarily caused by a routine bacterial UTI.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Chronic bladder pain syndrome occurs without an active identifiable infection.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Urinary frequency, bladder-associated pelvic pain, and typically smaller voided volumes persist for weeks.
- **Complications and consequences:** Chronic pain, sleep disruption, and reduced quality of life can occur.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Exclude infection and other urinary disorders with history and appropriate testing.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Individualized symptom management includes behavioral and other bladder-directed approaches.

**Urinary tract infection (UTI)** — Urinary slides 15-16

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Female anatomy, catheters, retention, diabetes, and urinary tract abnormalities elevate infection risk.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Organisms such as E. coli ascend and inflame urinary mucosa.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Dysuria, frequency, urgency, or upper-tract fever and flank pain vary by infection site.
- **Complications and consequences:** Ascending infection can cause pyelonephritis or sepsis.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Urinalysis and appropriately collected urine culture when indicated establish infection.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Targeted antibiotics when infection is symptomatic and clinically established.
- **Normal urinary defenses against bacterial infection:** The urinary tract is protected partly by urine flow, epithelial factors, local secretions, and normal flora; retention weakens clearance.

**Urethritis** — Urinary slide 15

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Bacterial pathogens including sexually transmitted organisms can inflame the urethra.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Infection or inflammation affects urethral mucosa rather than primarily renal parenchyma.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Dysuria and urethral discharge or irritation can occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Untreated sexually transmitted infections can cause transmission and other complications.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Targeted nucleic-acid testing is used when STI-associated urethritis is suspected.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Organism-appropriate antibiotics and partner management when indicated.

**Chronic pyelonephritis** — Urinary slide 20

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Recurrent ascending infection, reflux, or persistent urinary obstruction can produce chronic infection.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Repeated inflammation and scarring deform renal tissue and may obstruct bacterial clearance.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Flank pain, recurrent dysuria/frequency, and hypertension may be present.
- **Complications and consequences:** Chronic scarring can cause permanent renal impairment.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Urinalysis, culture, ultrasound, or other imaging assess recurrent infection and scarring.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Relieve obstructive causes and provide culture-directed prolonged treatment when needed.

### REN-4: Glomerular disease, AKI, and CKD (11 concepts)

**Nephrotic syndrome** — Urinary slide 24

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Nephrotic syndrome features marked urinary protein loss, low serum albumin, and edema.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Glomerular disease, diabetes, lupus, or certain drugs can cause nephrotic syndrome.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Increased glomerular permeability causes heavy proteinuria and loss of plasma albumin.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Massive edema, frothy urine, hyperlipidemia, and lipiduria are typical.
- **Complications and consequences:** Thrombosis and susceptibility to infection may increase with protein losses.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Heavy urine protein excretion and low serum albumin support the diagnosis.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Manage causative glomerular disease, sodium retention, and edema.

**Glomerulonephritis** — Urinary slides 22-23, 25

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Glomerulonephritis is inflammation of the glomeruli and may cause hematuria, proteinuria, edema, and hypertension.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Immune-mediated processes, postinfectious reactions, lupus, and other glomerular diseases may cause inflammation.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Glomerular inflammatory injury lowers filtration and allows blood and protein into urine.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Hematuria, proteinuria, edema, hypertension, and reduced GFR may be present.
- **Complications and consequences:** Severe cases progress to acute or chronic renal failure.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Urinalysis may reveal RBC casts; renal function tests and sometimes biopsy guide diagnosis.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Treat the underlying process and control fluid and blood-pressure complications.

**Acute kidney injury (AKI)** — Urinary slides 26-28

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** AKI involves an acute decline in filtration, commonly indicated by rising serum creatinine and/or low urine output.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Prerenal hypoperfusion, intrinsic renal injury, or postrenal obstruction are major categories.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** An abrupt fall in kidney function decreases filtration and excretion of solutes and fluid.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Rising creatinine, oliguria in some patients, and fluid or electrolyte abnormalities may appear.
- **Complications and consequences:** Hyperkalemia, pulmonary edema, acidosis, and uremic complications can develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Follow serum creatinine, urine output, electrolytes, and studies selected by the suspected cause.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Restore perfusion, discontinue nephrotoxins, and relieve obstruction when indicated.
- **Prerenal AKI mechanism:** Prerenal AKI is caused by decreased effective blood flow to the kidneys and may be reversible if corrected before ischemic injury.
- **Intrinsic AKI mechanism:** In intrinsic AKI the renal parenchyma itself is injured, unlike isolated hypoperfusion or outflow blockage.
- **Postrenal AKI mechanism:** Postrenal AKI arises distal to the filtering nephron from impaired urinary drainage, particularly with bilateral obstruction or a single functional kidney.
- **Clinical phases of acute kidney injury:** The class lecture distinguishes initiation, maintenance/oliguric, and recovery/polyuric phases; output alone does not guarantee full GFR recovery.

**Chronic kidney disease (CKD)** — Urinary slides 29-33

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Diabetes and hypertension are major contributors to chronic loss of renal function.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Diabetes and hypertension are leading contributors; lupus and prior kidney injury are other causes.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Progressive nephron loss reduces GFR, sodium balance, erythropoietin, and vitamin D activation.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Fatigue, edema, hypertension, nocturia, anemia, and metabolic abnormalities emerge as disease advances.
- **Complications and consequences:** Hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, bone-mineral disorder, and cardiovascular disease are important complications.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Persistently reduced eGFR or markers of kidney damage for 3 months support CKD.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Treat the cause, control blood pressure and complications, and consider renal replacement when indicated.
- **Why CKD causes mineral bone disease:** Declining renal phosphate excretion and vitamin D activation disrupt calcium/PTH balance, causing CKD-mineral and bone disorder.
- **Why CKD causes anemia:** The kidneys normally provide erythropoietin signals to red cell precursors; damaged kidneys produce less.
- **Chronic kidney disease staging by GFR:** The five GFR categories are G1 at least 90, G2 60–89, G3a 45–59, G3b 30–44, G4 15–29, and G5 below 15; chronicity and kidney-damage evidence are also considered.

**Hyperkalemia and cardiac rhythm complications** — Urinary slides 29, 31

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Reduced potassium excretion can lead to hyperkalemia and potentially dangerous rhythm disturbances.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Reduced kidney potassium excretion in advanced CKD or AKI can cause hyperkalemia.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Elevated extracellular potassium alters cardiac membrane excitability and conduction.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Weakness, palpitations, or ECG changes such as peaked T waves may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Dangerous dysrhythmias and cardiac arrest are possible.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Check serum potassium and ECG while excluding specimen hemolysis when appropriate.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Severe hyperkalemia requires urgent cardiac stabilization and potassium-lowering treatment.

**Poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis** — Urinary slide 25

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** A preceding streptococcal throat or skin infection can trigger postinfectious glomerular inflammation.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Immune-complex deposition activates complement and damages the glomerular capillaries.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Cola-colored urine, edema, hypertension, and reduced filtration may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Severe fluid overload or AKI can occur in acute cases.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Urine RBC casts, complement levels, and streptococcal serologies support diagnosis.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Support blood pressure/fluid balance and treat any ongoing infection.

**Metabolic acidosis in kidney failure** — Urinary slides 8, 29-31

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Advanced kidney dysfunction reduces capacity to remove daily acid loads.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Impaired ammonium generation and net acid excretion cause reduced serum bicarbonate.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Fatigue and deep compensatory breathing may occur in severe metabolic acidosis.
- **Complications and consequences:** Bone buffering, muscle catabolism, and worse cardiovascular status may develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Low serum bicarbonate with appropriate blood-gas findings supports the diagnosis.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Treat renal disease and consider alkali or dialysis depending on severity.

**Anemia of chronic kidney disease** — Urinary slides 29-33

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Progressive loss of functioning renal tissue reduces erythropoietin production.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Reduced erythropoietin decreases marrow red-cell generation.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Fatigue, pallor, and decreased exercise tolerance can accompany low hemoglobin.
- **Complications and consequences:** Tissue oxygen delivery and quality of life can worsen.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** CBC, iron studies, and renal evaluation help characterize CKD-associated anemia.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Correct iron deficiency and use erythropoiesis-stimulating therapy when appropriate.

**Polycystic kidney disease** — Urinary slide 28

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Inherited cyst-forming disorders can progressively disrupt renal tissue.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Expanding renal cysts compress normal nephrons and alter kidney structure.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Hypertension, flank pain, hematuria, and enlarged kidneys may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Progressive CKD, infection, and cyst bleeding may develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Family history and kidney imaging establish cystic patterns.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Control blood pressure and manage complications; specialized disease-modifying therapy may be appropriate.

**Hypertensive nephrosclerosis** — Urinary slides 22, 28-29

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Long-standing poorly controlled hypertension can injure renal small vessels.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Vascular thickening and narrowing impair perfusion and damage nephrons.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Gradual GFR decline, hypertension, and variable proteinuria may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Progressive chronic kidney disease may result.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Serial eGFR, urine protein, and blood pressure assessment characterize the process.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Control blood pressure and other cardiovascular/renal risk factors.

**Lupus nephritis** — Urinary slides 22, 24, 29

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Systemic lupus erythematosus may generate immune complexes that injure renal tissue.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Immune-complex glomerular inflammation can cause protein leakage and impaired filtration.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Proteinuria, hematuria, edema, and hypertension may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Uncontrolled inflammation can progress to nephrotic syndrome or kidney failure.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Urinalysis, protein quantification, renal function, and often biopsy guide classification.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Immunosuppressive therapy and kidney-protective management target renal inflammation.

### MSK-1: Bone structure and fracture healing (3 concepts)

**Stages of fracture healing** — Musculoskeletal slide 3

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Fracture healing proceeds through hematoma, inflammatory, reparative, and remodeling stages.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Trauma and other causes of bony disruption initiate healing.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Healing progresses from hematoma and inflammation to reparative callus and remodeling.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Initial pain and swelling gradually improve as stability and callus form.
- **Complications and consequences:** Poor circulation, infection, or inadequate immobilization can delay healing.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Serial radiographs and functional assessment monitor healing.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Immobilization, nutrition, and restoration of appropriate alignment support union.

**Osteoblasts and bone matrix production** — Musculoskeletal slides 6-7

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Osteoblasts form osteoid, while osteoclasts resorb bone.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Bone remodeling depends on coordinated osteoblast and osteoclast activity.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Osteoblasts lay down osteoid; osteoclasts resorb mineralized bone.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Normal remodeling supports skeletal repair and adaptation.
- **Complications and consequences:** Excess resorption relative to formation contributes to bone fragility.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Bone-density evaluation and selected metabolic studies assess bone health.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Correct nutritional deficits and manage disorders shifting resorption versus formation.

**Open fractures and infection risk** — Musculoskeletal slides 2, 8, 22

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Open fractures expose deep tissue to contamination and infection.
- **Causes and risk factors:** High-energy trauma may produce a break with skin or soft-tissue communication.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** A disrupted skin barrier exposes fractured bone and tissues to environmental contamination.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Open wound, deformity, bleeding, pain, and possible neurovascular deficits are findings.
- **Complications and consequences:** Infection, osteomyelitis, tissue damage, and impaired healing are key risks.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Assess neurovascular status and use imaging to define fracture anatomy.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Urgent antibiotics, irrigation/debridement, stabilization, and tetanus assessment are priorities.

### MSK-2: Compartment syndrome (2 concepts)

**Acute compartment syndrome: recognition** — Musculoskeletal slides 9-10

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** High pressure inside a closed fascial compartment impairs tissue perfusion; delay risks ischemic damage.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Fracture, edema, bleeding, crush injury, or a restrictive cast can raise compartment pressure.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Raised pressure within fascia impairs microvascular tissue perfusion and causes ischemia.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch, tense compartments, and paresthesia are early clues.
- **Complications and consequences:** Untreated ischemia causes nerve and muscle necrosis and permanent disability.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Urgent clinical assessment is essential; pressure measurement may support equivocal cases.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Release external constriction and obtain immediate surgical evaluation for fasciotomy.

**Compartment syndrome despite palpable pulses** — Musculoskeletal slides 9-10

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Pulselessness is often a late finding; pain with passive stretch and neurologic signs may appear earlier.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Swelling in a closed fascial space can injure tissue even if larger arteries remain open.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Microvascular perfusion can fail well before distal pulses disappear.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Severe pain with passive stretch and paresthesia can precede pallor or pulselessness.
- **Complications and consequences:** Waiting for pulse loss can delay treatment until substantial tissue necrosis has occurred.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Serial pain, sensation, motion, and compartment-tension assessments are more informative than pulses alone.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Treat suspected syndrome as an emergency even when distal pulses are present.

### MSK-3: Sprains, strains, and dislocations (4 concepts)

**Sprain versus strain (ligament injuries)** — Musculoskeletal slide 13

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Sprains injure ligaments; strains affect muscles or tendons.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Twisting a joint can injure a ligament, while overstretching a muscle-tendon unit causes a strain.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** A sprain is ligament damage; a strain involves muscle or tendon rather than a ligament.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Localized pain, swelling, bruising, and reduced joint function are common.
- **Complications and consequences:** Severe injuries may cause joint instability or prolonged weakness.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** History, examination, and selected imaging help distinguish ligament from tendon injury.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Protect injured tissues, control swelling and pain, and rehabilitate function.

**Subluxation (partial dislocation)** — Musculoskeletal slide 13

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Subluxation is an incomplete or partial dislocation.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Trauma or ligamentous laxity can partially displace a joint.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** The articular surfaces lose part, but not all, of their normal contact.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Joint pain, deformity, and reduced range of motion may appear.
- **Complications and consequences:** Recurrent instability or damage to adjacent nerves and vessels may occur.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Neurovascular assessment and radiographs evaluate joint alignment.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Timely reduction when indicated followed by stabilization and rehabilitation.

**Muscle or tendon strain** — Musculoskeletal slide 13

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Overuse or sudden overstretching can tear muscle or tendon fibers.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Microscopic to complete muscle/tendon disruption triggers local inflammatory pain.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Localized pain with contraction or stretch, weakness, and swelling occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Persistent weakness or reinjury may occur if healing is incomplete.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Clinical examination and imaging for severe injury identify the structure involved.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Relative rest followed by graded rehabilitation and strengthening.

**Complete joint dislocation** — Musculoskeletal slides 13-14

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Trauma or joint instability can completely displace articular surfaces.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** The ends of bones completely lose normal joint contact.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Marked deformity, pain, loss of motion, and sometimes numbness occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Nerve injury, blood vessel damage, and recurrent instability can follow.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Assess pulses and nerves, and use radiographs when indicated.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Prompt trained reduction, immobilization, and rehabilitation are priorities.

### MSK-4: Muscle breakdown and metabolic bone disease (4 concepts)

**Rhabdomyolysis and kidney risks** — Musculoskeletal slides 15-16

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Muscle breakdown releases myoglobin and electrolytes, placing the kidneys and cardiac rhythm at risk.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Crush trauma, prolonged immobilization, toxins, infection, and some drugs can trigger muscle breakdown.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Injured myocytes release myoglobin, creatine kinase, and potassium into circulation.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Muscle pain, weakness, and dark urine may occur, though the classic triad is not always present.
- **Complications and consequences:** Myoglobin-mediated kidney injury and hyperkalemia can be life-threatening.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Markedly elevated total creatine kinase, urinalysis, electrolytes, and renal tests are useful.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Prompt IV fluids when appropriate, urine-output monitoring, and treating hyperkalemia protect kidneys.

**Osteoporosis** — Musculoskeletal slides 17-18

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Osteoporosis involves reduced bone mass and abnormal microarchitecture with increased fracture risk.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Age, estrogen deficiency, corticosteroids, low calcium or vitamin D, and inactivity increase risk.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Bone resorption exceeds formation, lowering bone mineral density and strength.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Often silent until a fragility fracture, height loss, or vertebral deformity occurs.
- **Complications and consequences:** Hip and vertebral fractures can cause pain, disability, and deformity.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** DXA evaluates bone density; fracture-risk assessment helps guide care.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Weight-bearing activity, adequate calcium/vitamin D, fall prevention, and selected medication help.

**Parathyroid hormone and calcium** — Musculoskeletal slides 19-20

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** PTH raises serum calcium by effects on bone, kidney, and vitamin D activation.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Low serum calcium stimulates PTH; some malignancies can elevate PTH-related peptide.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** PTH increases serum calcium through bone resorption, renal handling, and vitamin D activation.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Sustained excess PTH can contribute to bone pain or weakness.
- **Complications and consequences:** Chronic excess bone resorption raises risk of pathologic fractures.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Assess serum calcium, phosphate, PTH, and related clinical context.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Treat the underlying endocrine or malignant cause of calcium dysregulation.

**Osteomalacia** — Musculoskeletal slide 21

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Osteomalacia is insufficient mineralization of osteoid in mature bone.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Vitamin D deficiency, malabsorption, or impaired phosphate availability can cause osteomalacia.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Failure to mineralize new osteoid leaves adult bone abnormally soft despite ongoing remodeling.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Diffuse bone pain, proximal weakness, waddling gait, and possible bowed legs may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Fragility or pseudofractures and mobility problems can result.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Vitamin D, phosphate, calcium, alkaline phosphatase, and clinical context inform diagnosis.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Restore vitamin D and mineral supply while treating malabsorption or other cause.

### MSK-5: Joint disorders and inflammatory conditions (6 concepts)

**Osteomyelitis and sequestrum** — Musculoskeletal slide 23

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Sequestrum is necrotic separated bone; involucrum is new bone forming around it.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Bacterial spread, penetrating injury, surgery, or contiguous infection can seed bone.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Bone infection causes inflammation and necrosis; a sequestrum is a separated fragment of dead bone.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Focal bone pain, warmth, fever, chills, or malaise may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Abscesses, sepsis, bone deformity, and chronic infection may develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Imaging, inflammatory markers, and ideally microbiologic cultures help guide treatment.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Prolonged targeted antibiotics and sometimes surgical debridement are required.

**Rheumatoid arthritis** — Musculoskeletal slides 25-26

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Rheumatoid arthritis is a systemic autoimmune inflammatory arthropathy often affecting symmetric small joints.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Autoimmune synovitis develops with genetic and environmental risk factors.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Chronic inflammatory synovial pannus damages cartilage and bone.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Symmetric small-joint pain, swelling, morning stiffness, and fatigue are typical.
- **Complications and consequences:** Erosions, deformity, disability, and systemic manifestations may develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Clinical pattern, inflammatory markers, rheumatoid factor, and anti-CCP support diagnosis.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Early disease-modifying antirheumatic therapy helps prevent structural damage.
- **Distinguishing inflammatory RA from mechanical OA:** RA is a systemic inflammatory synovial disease; OA is primarily a mechanical degenerative whole-joint process with shorter morning stiffness.

**Osteoarthritis** — Musculoskeletal slides 25, 27

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Osteoarthritis is primarily degenerative and is not a systemic autoimmune inflammatory condition.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Age, obesity, prior joint injury, and mechanical loading are important risks.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Progressive cartilage and whole-joint degeneration produce mechanical joint symptoms.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Activity-related pain, brief morning stiffness, crepitus, and reduced function occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Progressive deformity or restricted mobility can develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Clinical features and radiographic joint-space narrowing support diagnosis when needed.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Activity modification, strengthening, weight management, analgesia, and selected joint procedures help.

**Ankylosing spondylitis** — Musculoskeletal slides 28-29, 33

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Axial spondyloarthritis may begin at sacroiliac joints and cause inflammatory back stiffness.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Genetic susceptibility, often associated with HLA-B27, increases risk.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Inflammatory damage at sacroiliac/spinal entheses can heal by ossification and fusion.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Inflammatory back pain and morning stiffness worsen after rest and improve with movement.
- **Complications and consequences:** Progressive spinal rigidity, kyphosis, or reduced chest expansion may occur.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Sacroiliac imaging, inflammatory history, and specialist assessment support diagnosis.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Physical therapy, exercise, NSAIDs, and selected biologic therapies reduce burden.

**Gout and urate crystals** — Musculoskeletal slides 30-31

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Gout occurs when monosodium urate crystals provoke an intense inflammatory response.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Hyperuricemia from reduced urate excretion or increased purine breakdown predisposes to gout.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Monosodium urate crystals deposit in joints and elicit intense neutrophilic inflammation.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Sudden severe red hot swollen joint, often the first MTP joint, is characteristic.
- **Complications and consequences:** Tophi, recurrent attacks, erosions, and uric-acid stones may develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Joint-fluid analysis can demonstrate needle-shaped negatively birefringent urate crystals.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Treat acute inflammation and consider urate-lowering therapy for recurrent disease.

**Fibromyalgia** — Musculoskeletal slide 32

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Fibromyalgia is associated with altered pain processing and widespread pain without typical tissue inflammation.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Mechanism is multifactorial and not explained by destructive inflammatory joint disease.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Altered central pain processing amplifies pain sensitivity despite no primary tissue inflammation.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Widespread pain, fatigue, poor sleep, and cognitive complaints are common.
- **Complications and consequences:** Reduced function and quality of life occur without typical erosive joint destruction.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Diagnosis is clinical after assessing competing causes of widespread pain.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Graded activity, sleep strategies, psychological support, and selected medications help.

### GI-8: GI assessments, nutrition, and surgical complications (4 concepts)

**GI bleeding and stool/vomitus appearance** — Digestive slide 5

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Ulcers, varices, inflammation, and tumors can bleed into the GI tract.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Digestion of upper GI blood can produce melena; rapid bleeding may cause hematemesis.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Coffee-ground or bloody emesis, black tarry stool, or fresh rectal blood require evaluation.
- **Complications and consequences:** Significant bleeding can cause anemia and hypovolemic shock.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Assess vitals, hemoglobin, stool/emesis appearance, and source-directed testing.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Stabilize hemodynamics and investigate/treat bleeding source promptly.

**Gastrectomy complications** — Digestive slide 17

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Gastric cancer or refractory complicated ulcer disease may require partial or total gastric resection.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Loss of gastric reservoir and altered emptying can trigger rapid intestinal delivery of food.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Post-meal cramping, diarrhea, lightheadedness, or palpitations may indicate dumping syndrome.
- **Complications and consequences:** Vitamin B12 deficiency after total gastrectomy and nutritional deficiencies are important risks.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Review nutritional status, symptoms after meals, and relevant blood tests.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Smaller meals and targeted nutritional replacement, including B12 when indicated, are needed.

**Celiac disease and malabsorption** — Digestive slides 5, 28

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Gluten exposure in a genetically susceptible individual triggers immune-mediated small-bowel injury.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Immune damage to villi reduces absorptive surface and nutrient uptake.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Chronic diarrhea, steatorrhea, bloating, iron deficiency, and weight loss may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Untreated malabsorption can lead to vitamin deficits and reduced bone density.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Tissue-transglutaminase IgA with total IgA and small-bowel biopsy when indicated support diagnosis.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** A strict gluten-free diet prevents ongoing injury.

**Cystic fibrosis and pancreatic insufficiency** — Digestive slide 5

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Inherited CFTR dysfunction affects epithelial salt and water movement, producing thick secretions.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Thick pancreatic secretions obstruct ducts and can impair enzyme delivery into the bowel.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Steatorrhea, poor growth, and fat-soluble vitamin deficiency may accompany pancreatic insufficiency.
- **Complications and consequences:** Malnutrition and vitamin deficiencies may follow if not treated.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Clinical and genetic evaluation plus fecal elastase can identify pancreatic insufficiency.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Pancreatic enzyme replacement and nutritional support treat malabsorption.

### GI-5: Hepatitis, fibrosis, and liver disease (7 concepts)

**Hepatitis A** — Digestive slides 29-30

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** HAV is transmitted primarily by the fecal-oral route through contaminated food, water, or close contact.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Acute viral hepatocyte inflammation can cause a prodromal illness followed by jaundice.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Fatigue, anorexia, nausea, dark urine, pale stool, and jaundice may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Occasional severe acute liver failure is possible, but chronic HAV infection does not occur.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** HAV IgM supports diagnosis of recent infection.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Supportive care, hygiene, and HAV vaccination for prevention are key.

**Hepatitis B** — Digestive slides 29-30

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** HBV spreads through blood, sexual exposure, and perinatal transmission.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** HBV can cause acute hepatitis and persist chronically, provoking fibrosis.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Fatigue, malaise, anorexia, dark urine, and jaundice may appear.
- **Complications and consequences:** Chronic HBV raises cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma risk.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** HBsAg and other HBV serologies define infection status; HBV DNA assesses replication.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Vaccination prevents infection and antiviral therapy may control chronic HBV.
- **Hepatitis B and D dependence:** HDV depends on HBV envelope proteins, so protection from HBV through vaccination also prevents HDV acquisition.

**Hepatitis C** — Digestive slides 29-30

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** HCV is primarily bloodborne, especially through shared injection equipment.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Persistent HCV infection may produce chronic hepatic inflammation and fibrosis.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Many are initially asymptomatic; fatigue or signs of chronic liver injury may develop.
- **Complications and consequences:** Cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma are long-term risks.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Screen with HCV antibody followed by HCV RNA confirmation.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Direct-acting antivirals can cure most HCV infections; no vaccine is available.

**Hepatitis D** — Digestive slides 29-30

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** HDV requires concurrent HBV infection because it uses hepatitis B surface antigen.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** HDV coinfection or superinfection can amplify hepatitis severity.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Features resemble viral hepatitis, including fatigue, nausea, and jaundice.
- **Complications and consequences:** Severe hepatitis and accelerated cirrhosis are concerns, especially with superinfection.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Check HDV testing when indicated in an HBV-infected patient.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Prevent HBV through vaccination; specialist management is used for HDV infection.

**Hepatitis E** — Digestive slides 29-30

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** HEV is usually transmitted by the fecal-oral route in contaminated water or food.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Typically causes acute hepatitis rather than persistent disease in immunocompetent hosts.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Fatigue, abdominal symptoms, jaundice, and dark urine may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Severe illness is a particular concern in pregnancy; chronic infection can occur in some immunosuppressed patients.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** HEV serology or nucleic-acid tests may help confirm when available.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Supportive care and safe water/food sanitation are important.

**Cirrhosis** — Digestive slides 31-35

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Chronic alcohol use, chronic HBV/HCV, steatotic liver disease, autoimmune injury, toxins, and inherited disorders can cause cirrhosis.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Repeated hepatocyte injury produces diffuse fibrosis and regenerative nodules that distort hepatic blood and bile flow.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Fatigue, anorexia, weight loss, anemia, pruritus, jaundice, easy bruising, and abdominal discomfort can occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Portal hypertension, esophageal varices, ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, and coagulopathy may develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Evaluate liver tests, bilirubin, albumin, INR, platelets, and imaging; elastography can assess fibrosis.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Stop ongoing liver injury; manage complications and evaluate transplant candidacy in decompensated disease.
- **Why cirrhosis produces ascites:** Portal hypertension, reduced effective arterial volume, renal salt retention, and reduced oncotic pressure jointly produce cirrhotic ascites.
- **Why cirrhosis causes esophageal varices:** High portal pressure forces flow through lower-pressure collateral pathways including gastroesophageal veins.
- **Why cirrhosis increases bleeding risk:** Cirrhosis disrupts a complex balance of pro- and anticoagulant factors; decreased synthetic function and low platelets contribute to bleeding risk.
- **Why cirrhosis alters mental status:** Blood bypassing damaged liver tissue and impaired hepatocyte clearance allow neurotoxic substances to reach the brain.

**Jaundice and bilirubin handling** — Digestive slides 2, 31-32

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Hepatocellular injury, impaired bile drainage, or hemolysis can elevate bilirubin.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Bilirubin accumulation in tissue produces yellow discoloration of skin and sclera.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Yellow sclera/skin, pruritus, dark urine, and sometimes pale stools occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Persistently impaired bile flow can cause fat-soluble vitamin malabsorption.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Fractionated bilirubin and liver biochemical tests help identify the pattern.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Treat the hepatocellular, biliary, or hemolytic cause rather than jaundice alone.

### GI-6: Biliary and pancreatic disease (3 concepts)

**Cholelithiasis (gallstones)** — Digestive slide 39

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Female sex, obesity, and pregnancy are associated with cholesterol gallstone risk.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Bile components crystallize into stones; transient duct obstruction causes biliary colic.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Episodic right upper quadrant pain after fatty meals, sometimes radiating to the shoulder, may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Cholecystitis, common duct obstruction, or gallstone pancreatitis may develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Right upper quadrant ultrasound is a common first-line test.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Asymptomatic stones often need no treatment; symptomatic stones may need cholecystectomy.

**Acute cholecystitis** — Digestive slide 39

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Gallstone impaction in the cystic duct is the usual trigger of acute calculous cholecystitis.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Persistent cystic duct obstruction distends and inflames the gallbladder.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Right upper quadrant pain lasting hours, fever, nausea, and Murphy sign may occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Gangrene, perforation, and infection can complicate untreated inflammation.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Ultrasound can show gallstones and wall changes; HIDA testing may clarify uncertain cases.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** IV fluids, pain control, appropriate antibiotics, and often early cholecystectomy.

**Acute pancreatitis** — Digestive slide 40

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Gallstones and heavy alcohol use are major causes of acute pancreatitis.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Premature activation of pancreatic enzymes leads to autodigestion and inflammation.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, nausea, and vomiting are typical.
- **Complications and consequences:** Necrosis, hypovolemia, systemic inflammatory response, and organ failure can develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Lipase elevation and clinical findings help diagnose; imaging is used selectively.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** IV fluid resuscitation, analgesia, nutrition as tolerated, and treatment of the cause are key.

### GI-7: Gastrointestinal and hepatobiliary cancers (5 concepts)

**Colorectal cancer** — Digestive slides 41, 44

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Age, hereditary polyposis, long-standing ulcerative colitis, and dietary or lifestyle factors increase risk.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Adenomatous polyps can develop mutations over time and progress to invasive carcinoma.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Rectal bleeding, changed bowel habits, unexplained anemia, fatigue, and weight loss are warning signs.
- **Complications and consequences:** Obstruction, chronic bleeding, and metastatic spread may occur.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Colonoscopy with biopsy establishes a lesion; staging directs treatment.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Surgical removal with stage-appropriate systemic or radiation treatment.

**Pancreatic adenocarcinoma** — Digestive slide 41

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Smoking, chronic pancreatitis, and genetic susceptibility are risk factors.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Ductal epithelial malignancy invades adjacent structures and often spreads before diagnosis.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Weight loss, abdominal or back pain, and painless jaundice with head-of-pancreas tumors can occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Early metastasis and biliary obstruction are major risks.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Pancreas-protocol CT and tissue evaluation guide diagnosis and staging.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Surgical resection is possible in select early cases; systemic therapy is commonly needed.

**Hepatocellular carcinoma** — Digestive slide 42

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Cirrhosis, chronic HBV/HCV, and metabolic liver disease increase risk.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Malignant hepatocytes form a primary liver cancer, often on a cirrhotic background.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Early symptoms may be vague; later weight loss, pain, and liver decompensation can occur.
- **Complications and consequences:** Vascular invasion, spread, and worsening liver failure can develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Liver imaging with characteristic enhancement and/or biopsy when needed guides diagnosis.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Treatment options include resection, transplant in eligible cases, ablation, and systemic therapy.

**Gastric cancer** — Digestive slide 43

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** H. pylori, certain dietary exposures, and hereditary factors increase gastric cancer risk.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Chronic mucosal injury and dysplasia may progress to gastric adenocarcinoma.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Early satiety, anorexia, indigestion, fatigue, weight loss, or iron-deficiency anemia can appear.
- **Complications and consequences:** Occult bleeding, obstruction, and metastatic spread may occur.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Upper endoscopy with tissue biopsy is required to diagnose a suspicious lesion.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Stage-directed surgery and systemic therapy are main approaches.

**Metastatic cancer involving the liver** — Digestive slide 42

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Tumors from colorectal and other organs can spread to the liver through blood or lymphatic routes.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Secondary liver lesions arise from a cancer that originated outside the liver.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Weight loss, abdominal discomfort, or abnormal liver tests may appear late.
- **Complications and consequences:** Increased hepatic tumor burden can impair liver function.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Imaging and tissue or tumor history determine the primary source.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Treat according to primary cancer and extent of metastatic disease.

### REN-5: Renal diagnostics, urine retention, and dialysis (1 concepts)

**Dialysis: hemodialysis versus peritoneal** — Urinary slide 34

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Severe kidney failure or selected life-threatening electrolyte/volume problems may require dialysis.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Hemodialysis uses an extracorporeal filter; peritoneal dialysis exchanges solutes across the peritoneum.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Fluid overload, dangerous potassium, acidosis, or uremic findings may signal need.
- **Complications and consequences:** Hemodialysis access complications and peritonitis with peritoneal dialysis are important risks.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Monitor electrolytes, fluid status, access condition, and treatment response.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Dialysis removes selected wastes and fluid while kidney replacement plans are made.

### MSK-6: General fracture types and healing complications (3 concepts)

**Fractures: causes and classification** — Musculoskeletal slides 2, 4

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Trauma, osteoporosis, and pathologic bone lesions can cause fractures.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Disrupted bone initiates bleeding, hematoma, inflammatory signaling, and repair.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Pain, swelling, deformity, crepitus, or impaired motion may be present.
- **Complications and consequences:** Neurovascular injury, infection, and fat embolism can complicate selected fractures.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Assess distal circulation and nerves; use X-ray for fracture classification.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Realignment and immobilization by reduction, traction, or fixation are options.
- **Distinguishing fracture classifications:** The lecture diagram contrasts comminuted, oblique, spiral, transverse, greenstick, and open fracture patterns.

**Delayed union, nonunion, and malunion** — Musculoskeletal slide 5

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Infection, impaired perfusion, instability, or nutritional problems can delay repair.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Nonunion fails to bridge, delayed union heals slower than expected, and malunion heals in poor alignment.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Persistent pain or abnormal alignment after the expected period can suggest impaired union.
- **Complications and consequences:** Deformity, disability, and later osteoarthritis may occur.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Serial imaging and assessment of stability determine healing progress.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Address infection or instability, and consider bone grafting/revision where indicated.

**Fat embolism syndrome after fracture** — Musculoskeletal slide 5

- **Core recognition and interpretation:** Identify the condition from its manifestations and mechanisms.
- **Causes and risk factors:** Long-bone or pelvic fracture can release marrow fat into blood.
- **Pathophysiology and mechanisms:** Embolized fat and inflammation disrupt pulmonary and systemic microcirculation.
- **Clinical manifestations:** Respiratory distress, neurologic changes, and petechiae may appear.
- **Complications and consequences:** Hypoxemia and multi-organ dysfunction can develop.
- **Assessment and diagnostics:** Clinical recognition after a high-risk fracture and oxygenation assessment are key.
- **Treatment and nursing priorities:** Support oxygenation and hemodynamics; stabilize fractures early.
